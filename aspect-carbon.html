Carbon & Rocket Exhaust

A commercial launch lasts minutes. Its environmental footprint begins earlier and reaches far higher than the visible plume.

NASA Earth Observatory (2001), Smoke Plume
01

Suborbital tourism has an unusually high emissions intensity because each flight combines energy-intensive propellant production with rocket combustion for only a small number of passengers.

A 2025 peer-reviewed modelling study estimated current suborbital flights at roughly 85–226 tonnes of CO₂ per trip, depending on vehicle design and fuel choice. The analysis found that traditional carbon-based propellants such as kerosene and solid fuels generally produce more CO₂ than hydrogen- or methane-based systems.

Hydrogen illustrates why life-cycle accounting matters. Burning liquid hydrogen does not directly release carbon dioxide, but producing the fuel still carries a large indirect footprint. The same 2025 study estimated roughly 90–106 tonnes of indirect CO₂ from hydrogen production for the vehicles it analysed.

When emissions are normalized by passenger and flight duration, the contrast with ordinary aviation becomes much sharper. The study estimates suborbital tourism at around 400–1,000 times the CO₂ emissions per passenger-hour of commercial flights. Under a high-demand growth scenario, annual emissions could rise to about 21 million tonnes of CO₂ within a decade.

A separate life-cycle assessment of human space travel reached the same broader conclusion from another angle, estimating roughly 1.5–3.5 tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per person-hour for sustaining humans in space once wider system impacts are considered.

Research story

The launch is only the visible part of the footprint.

The research points to a chain that begins before ignition and continues after the plume has disappeared from view.

01

Propellant production

Fuel can carry a substantial upstream energy cost. Cleaner-looking exhaust does not automatically mean a clean life cycle.

02

Launch combustion

The engine produces carbon dioxide alongside water vapour, nitrogen compounds, soot and other products during ascent.

03

High-altitude release

Rockets transport those emissions vertically through the atmosphere, placing pollutants in regions where they can behave differently from emissions near the surface.

The takeaway.

Space tourism's carbon problem is not a single plume. It is a production-to-atmosphere system whose climate burden is unusually large for the number of passengers served.
