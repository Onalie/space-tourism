Stratospheric Ozone Depletion

The stratosphere is not empty space on the way to orbit. It contains a protective system that rocket emissions can disturb.

NASA Earth Observatory (2003), Viewing Earth's Limb
02

The ozone issue is not simply that rockets pass through the stratosphere. Their exhaust contains chemically and radiatively active species that can interact with a protective layer still recovering from twentieth-century damage.

A 2025 chemistry-climate study examined two near-future launch-growth scenarios. Its conservative case modelled 884 launches per year, while its ambitious case used 2,040 launches per year. Both scenarios produced near-global ozone losses, showing that continued launch growth can become large enough to influence recovery at a planetary scale.

The modelled annual-mean near-global ozone reduction was about 0.17% in the conservative case and 0.29% in the ambitious case. The stronger regional signal appeared over Antarctica in spring, where the ambitious scenario produced a 3.9% ozone decrease.

The chemistry depends on propellant choice. Modern rockets can emit carbon dioxide, water vapour, black carbon, nitrogen oxides, alumina particles and reactive chlorine-containing species. Solid rocket motors and black-carbon-producing propellants are especially important in the modelling because they can influence ozone through both chemistry and stratospheric heating.

This matters because ozone recovery is unfinished. The WMO/UNEP assessment projects a return to 1980 ozone levels around 2040 for the near-global mean, around 2045 in the Arctic and around 2066 over Antarctica if current protective policies remain in place. New launch-related pressures therefore arrive during a recovery process measured in decades.

Research story

The recovery we could interrupt.

The environmental stakes become clearer when launch growth is placed beside the long international effort to restore stratospheric ozone.

01

A protective atmospheric layer

Stratospheric ozone absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation. It is not simply a corridor that rockets pass through without consequence.

02

Pollution stays longer at altitude

Once emissions reach the stratosphere, they can remain far longer than many pollutants near the ground, increasing their opportunity to participate in chemical reactions.

03

Frequency turns single launches into cumulative pressure

A growing tourism industry would repeatedly add new pollution to the same atmospheric layer, shifting the issue from isolated events to a recurring environmental load.

The takeaway.

The ozone risk is not dramatic because one rocket crosses the stratosphere. It becomes serious when repeated launches keep adding persistent pollutants to a protective layer that took decades to recover.
